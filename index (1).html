Between Languages

An interpretivist proposal on university students in Kazakhstan who have shifted to Kazakh as their primary language, 2025–2026.

PLS 210 2025–2026
01

Research question

"How do university students in Kazakhstan who have shifted to Kazakh as their primary language describe this shift during 2025–2026?"

  • Empirical, not normative — what students say, not what they should do
  • Population — students who have already shifted, not the general student body
  • Context / time — universities in Kazakhstan, 2025–2026
02

Epistemological positioning

This study sits in the interpretivist tradition.

  • Social reality is constructed through how people interpret their own experience (Schwartz-Shea & Yanow, 2012)
  • Language shift is bound to identity and meaning — not reducible to a proficiency score
  • The question asks what the shift means to the people living it
03

Contribution

Empirical

Fills the gap: student narratives are missing from work centred on policy (Kucherbayeva & Smagulova, 2023; Zhunussova et al., 2023).

Comparative

Speaks to international language revitalisation research (Warditz et al., 2026).

Methodological

Demonstrates an interpretive approach in a field dominated by positivist work.

04
ideology, not choice

Literature review

Language reads as ideology, not preference: Russian = prestige, Kazakh = authenticity.

AHigher education, medium of instruction, identity

Kucherbayeva & Smagulova (2023); Zhunussova et al. (2023)

Focus
Kazakh-medium instruction, English-medium instruction, language policy, student identity and ideology
Participants / method
University students in Astana and across Kazakhstan; qualitative interviews and focus groups
Relevance
Closest to this question — how university students narrate studying in Kazakh or EMI, and how they talk about identity and structural constraints
BEarly socialisation and developmental foundations

Smagulova (2013); Kyuchuk et al. (2025)

Focus
"Baby talk," caregiver language choice, preschool narratives, theory of mind, early bilingualism
Participants / method
Urban Kazakh families in Almaty; preschool children
Relevance
Explains the early patterns behind later shift or reversal — why Russian often becomes the language of agency while Kazakh is confined to affect and infancy
CStructural and grammatical consequences of reversal

Warditz et al. (2026)

Focus
Aspectuality, nativeness, heritage Kazakh, reversal of language shift
Participants / method
95 participants, mostly university students in Almaty; quantitative survey
Relevance
Shows what happens to core Kazakh grammar when speakers shift back from Russian dominance — useful for "nativeness" and variability in students' Kazakh

The gap: how students who shifted to Kazakh describe that choice, in their own words, 2025–2026.

05
starting points, not predictions

Analytical framework

Sensitising concepts — orient the study without predetermining findings.

i.

Reverse language shift

An ongoing, uneven process — not a direct policy effect (Warditz et al., 2026).

ii.

Language beliefs and values

How students weigh Kazakh vs. Russian across settings (Smagulova, 2013; Kucherbayeva & Smagulova, 2023).

iii.

Language shift as ideological arena

Choices as part of larger power relations — Soviet legacy, nation-building (Kucherbayeva & Smagulova, 2023).

06
assumptions, held loosely

Expectations

i.

Decolonial choice, personal activism, or both

How students may frame the shift.

ii.

Policy vs. lived experience

Contradictions between official language policy and what students actually experience.

iii.

Diverse voices

Responses may be both similar and contradictory.

iv.

Open to surprise

An abductive approach — questions or methods may shift once I start listening.

07
inside, not outside

Positionality

I am not a neutral observer.

i.

Who I am

Grew up in a bilingual environment; resident of Kazakhstan.

ii.

Inside the hierarchies

I am part of the same linguistic hierarchies I study.

iii.

The risk

My position may shape what I notice and how I read participants' words.

iv.

What I will do

Reflect throughout on how my position influences data collection and the final results.
